The journey of a dotHow a plane gets onto your screen

Every aircraft on the radar is announcing itself. Nothing is being bounced off it, and there is no big spinning dish involved. The system is called ADS-B (Automatic Dependent Surveillance–Broadcast).

  1. The plane works out where it isIt uses satellite navigation (GPS) to know its own position, altitude and speed.
  2. It shouts that out, over and overA transponder broadcasts the position about twice a second on 1090 MHz, along with the plane's unique 24-bit ID, its callsign and its speed.
  3. A receiver on the ground hears itMany are run by hobbyists with a small radio dongle and an antenna. Radio like this travels in straight lines, so a plane at cruise height can be heard from a few hundred miles away.
  4. The data is shared onlineReceivers send what they hear to community networks, which combine it into one live picture of the sky.
  5. Your radar asks for the nearby partAero Sentry requests the planes around your location, then draws each one at the right bearing and distance.
Try it: why the app predicts where a plane is

A plane's position only updates every second or so, and the data takes a moment to reach you. So the radar uses dead reckoning: take the last known position, then move it along the plane's heading at its speed.

Gaps in the skyWhy isn't every plane on the map?

Some aircraft don't broadcast
Gliders, balloons and many older or very light aircraft have no ADS-B transponder at all, so a radar built on ADS-B can't see them.
Military aircraft can stay quiet
Military flights may switch their transponder off or not share identifying details. That's why a rare military contact showing up is worth noticing.
Line of sight and low flying
Radio signals can be blocked by hills, buildings and the curve of the Earth. A plane flying low, far from any receiver, may be too quiet to hear.
Data feeds can filter
Some networks hide certain aircraft on request. Different feeds can show slightly different skies.

Reading the dataSquawk codes

A squawk is a four-digit code pilots set so air traffic control can pick them out. Three are special:

7700General emergency – something is wrong on board
7600Radio failure – the pilots can't talk to ATC
7500Hijack – unlawful interference

AltitudeWhat is a flight level?

Above a certain height, pilots set their altimeters to one standard air pressure (1013.25 hPa) so everyone shares the same reference. Heights are then quoted in hundreds of feet: FL350 means 35,000 ft. Most jet airliners cruise somewhere between roughly FL300 and FL400.

Flat map, round EarthWhy do long flights curve?

The shortest route between two points on a sphere is a great circle. Drawn on a flat map it looks like a curve, which is why flights from Europe to North America often arc towards the north even though they're flying the shortest way.

TimelineA short history of flight

  • 1903The Wright brothers make the first powered, controlled flight: 12 seconds, 120 feet.
  • 1919Alcock and Brown fly non-stop across the Atlantic. The same year, the first daily international scheduled air service begins between London and Paris, and KLM is founded.
  • 1952The de Havilland Comet enters service with BOAC as the world's first jet airliner.
  • 1958Pan Am starts transatlantic services with the Boeing 707, making jet travel across the ocean routine.
  • 1970The Boeing 747 enters service, the first “jumbo jet”.
  • 1976Concorde begins passenger flights with British Airways and Air France, cruising at twice the speed of sound.
  • 2003Concorde makes its final flights.
  • 2007The Airbus A380 enters service with Singapore Airlines.
  • 2011The Boeing 787 Dreamliner enters service with All Nippon Airways.
  • 2020ADS-B Out becomes required for most aircraft in controlled airspace in the US, which is part of why open radars like this one can exist.

Planes you might spotFamous aircraft

B744 · FIRST FLEW 1969

Boeing 747

Nicknamed the “Queen of the Skies”. Four engines and a hump at the front holding an upper deck. Easy to recognise on a clear day.
A388 · FIRST FLEW 2005

Airbus A380

The only airliner with two full-length passenger decks. Four engines and a huge wing. The last one was delivered in 2021.
B738 · FIRST FLEW 1967 (737 FAMILY)

Boeing 737

One of the most-built airliners ever. If you see a twin-engined short-haul jet with a slightly flattened engine nacelle, it's often a 737.
A320 · FIRST FLEW 1987

Airbus A320 family

The main rival to the 737 on short and medium routes. Fly-by-wire controls were a big step for airliners when it arrived.
CONC · SERVICE 1976–2003

Concorde

Delta wing, droop nose, Mach 2. It could cross the Atlantic in under four hours. Nothing has replaced it in passenger service.
B788 · FIRST FLEW 2009

Boeing 787 Dreamliner

Built largely from carbon-fibre composite, with big windows, wings that flex upward in flight, and zig-zag edges on the engine covers.
